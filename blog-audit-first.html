How We Audit a Business
Before We Build Anything

Every engagement starts with an audit — not a proposal. Here is exactly what we look at.

HT
Harry Thornberry Co-Founder, 1760 Strategic AI

We do not send proposals. We run audits. This is not a semantic distinction — it reflects a fundamental difference in how we think about what a consulting engagement is supposed to accomplish. A proposal starts with what we want to sell. An audit starts with what your business actually needs. Those two things are rarely the same, and conflating them is the reason most consulting relationships produce underwhelming results.

Every engagement we take on begins with a 30-minute diagnostic conversation. Not a discovery call. Not a sales call. A structured audit with a defined framework, specific questions, and a clear output: a diagnosis of where the business is constrained and a prioritized set of interventions that would move it forward. The audit drives the scope of the engagement. We never scope work we have not audited first.

Here is exactly what we look at.

Area one: Revenue

The first area we examine is revenue — not the number itself, but the system that generates it. We want to understand how revenue is currently being created, how dependent it is on the founder's direct involvement, and where the structural vulnerabilities are.

The questions we ask in the revenue audit are direct. Where does new business come from, and how would that change if the founder was unavailable for 30 days? What does the pipeline look like right now, and how was it built? What is the conversion rate from first conversation to signed engagement, and does anyone other than the founder know that number? Is revenue growing, and if so, is it growing in a way that is sustainable or in a way that is adding disproportionate operational burden?

What we are looking for is the difference between revenue that the business generates and revenue that the founder generates. For most service businesses we audit, the gap between those two things is much larger than the founder realizes — and closing that gap is the single highest-leverage intervention available.

Area two: Operations

The second area is operations — how the business actually delivers what it sells. We are specifically looking for single points of failure: the places where the business is dependent on an individual's knowledge, judgment, or availability rather than a documented, repeatable process.

We ask: if your most operationally critical team member left tomorrow, what would break first and how long would it take to notice? What is your onboarding process for a new client, and is it written down anywhere? How do you know when a delivery is going well versus when it is quietly going sideways? Where does quality assurance happen, and who is responsible for it?

"In every audit we have run, the biggest operational risk is not the person leadership is worried about losing. It is the process that was never documented because the person doing it made it look effortless."

The operations audit rarely produces surprises for the founder — most already sense where the fragility is. What the audit produces is clarity: a ranked list of the vulnerabilities, ordered by the severity of the impact if they fail, so that the remediation effort can be prioritized correctly rather than addressing the most visible problem first.

Area three: Intelligence

The third area is intelligence — how the founder currently makes decisions, and whether the information infrastructure supports good decision-making or undermines it. This is the area most founders find most uncomfortable to examine, because it requires acknowledging the gap between how decisions are supposed to be made and how they are actually made.

We ask: what are the three decisions you find hardest to make well right now? When you made a significant business decision in the last six months, what data did you use to make it? What would you need to know to make that decision with more confidence? Are there problems you should have seen coming that you only recognized after they had already materialized?

The intelligence audit reveals whether the founder is operating with genuine visibility into the business or whether they are making high-stakes decisions based on incomplete, lagging, or incorrectly structured information. Most founders, when asked these questions directly, recognize immediately that their intelligence infrastructure is not matching the complexity of the decisions they need to make.

What the audit output looks like

At the end of the 30-minute audit, we produce a short written output — not a 40-page report, not a proposal deck. A one-page diagnostic with three sections:

  • The primary constraint — The single most important thing limiting the business's ability to grow or operate sustainably. There is always one thing that, if addressed, unlocks everything else. The audit identifies it.
  • The ranked interventions — The three to five specific changes that would have the most impact, ordered by leverage and urgency. These interventions become the scope of any engagement we take on.
  • The honest assessment — Our view of whether and how we can help, including the things we would not recommend and the areas where the founder would be better served by a different kind of support.

This output drives everything that follows. If we take on an engagement, the scope is built directly from the audit output. If we do not take on an engagement, the audit output still gives the founder something actionable — a clear diagnosis they can take into any other conversation or address on their own.

Key Takeaways

  • Audit before you build — scoping work without diagnosis produces solutions to the wrong problems
  • The three audit areas are revenue (how it is generated and by whom), operations (where the single points of failure are), and intelligence (how decisions are actually being made)
  • The audit output is a one-page diagnostic: primary constraint, ranked interventions, honest assessment
  • The audit scope drives the engagement scope — every intervention we recommend has a direct line back to a diagnosed constraint

Why we do not skip the audit

We have been asked more than once to skip the audit and go straight to an engagement. The ask usually comes with a plausible rationale: the founder already knows what they need, the problem is obvious, the audit is a formality. We decline every time.

The audit is not a formality. It is the mechanism that prevents us from building the wrong thing. Every time we have been tempted to skip it, the audit — when we did eventually run it — revealed a constraint or a context that would have significantly changed our recommendation. The 30 minutes the audit takes is the highest-leverage investment in any engagement. We have never once regretted running it.

If you are ready to know exactly where your business is constrained and what to do about it, book a 30-minute AI Audit. That conversation is the audit. No pitch, no proposal — just the diagnostic.
